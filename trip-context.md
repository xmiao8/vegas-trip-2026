DEC 24 · ~45 MIN EACH WAY
Hoover Dam & Lake Mead

A 1930s concrete giant, the reservoir behind it, and a moonrise on Christmas Eve.

Hoover Dam holds back the Colorado River to form Lake Mead, the largest reservoir in the US. The lake is now near record lows, so a band of bleached white rock — the “bathtub ring” — shows how far the water has dropped. The dam sits on the Nevada–Arizona line; in winter the Arizona side is an hour ahead.

Expect security screening for vehicles approaching the dam.
Christmas Eve hours may be shorter — do the visitor center early.

Sphere

The giant LED dome next to the Venetian — free to see outside, wild to see inside.

Sphere opened in 2023. Its outside is covered in LEDs and shows changing animations every night, visible from much of the Strip. During your trip it’s showing The Wizard of Oz at Sphere, a re-imagined version of the 1939 film made for the dome, plus concert residencies.

Zion National Park

A deep red-rock canyon with the Virgin River running through it.

Zion Canyon is lined with sandstone cliffs up to 2,000 feet high, with easy riverside trails on the canyon floor and steeper climbs to viewpoints. In late December it’s quiet and cold — highs around the 50s °F, nights near freezing — and a dusting of snow on the red cliffs is common and beautiful. It’s in Utah, an hour ahead of Las Vegas.

ONE OF DEC 28–30 · ~2 HR EACH WAY
Death Valley National Park

Salt flats, badlands and dunes by day; one of the darkest skies in the country at night.

The hottest, driest and lowest national park in the US, and the largest outside Alaska. In winter it’s pleasant — highs in the 60s–70s °F — and the scenery changes every few miles: a white salt flat 282 feet below sea level, golden badlands, multicolored hills, sand dunes and 5,000-foot viewpoints. After dark, it’s a certified dark-sky park, which is why your stargazing night is here.

What you’ll see
Saturn
High in the south-southwest after dark

A steady, yellowish “star.” Through binoculars it’s just a dot, but it’s easy to find.

Jupiter
Rises in the east around 9–10 p.m.

The brightest thing in the sky after the moon. Binoculars held steady show up to four of its moons as tiny dots.

Mars
Near Jupiter in the east, later in the evening

Reddish and fairly bright.

Orion
Rises in the east after dark

The three-star belt, and the Orion Nebula as a fuzzy patch below it — visible to the eye under a dark sky.

The Pleiades
High in the east-southeast early evening

A tight little cluster of stars; stunning in binoculars.

The winter Milky Way
Overhead and to the east

Fainter than the summer Milky Way, but clearly visible from Death Valley once your eyes adjust.

Stargazing tips
Give your eyes 20–30 minutes in the dark, and use only a red light — one white phone screen resets them.
Good spots: Harmony Borax Works, the Mesquite Flat dunes, or Badwater Basin. Avoid the lights around Furnace Creek.
It gets cold fast: expect the 30s–40s °F. Bring a warm jacket, hat and gloves, and something to sit on.
A free sky app (like Stellarium) helps you name what you’re seeing.
Turn off headlights when you park at a viewing spot, and park well off the road.
If the forecast is cloudy on all three nights, the night with thin high clouds is still worth it — bright planets and stars shine through.
